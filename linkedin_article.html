A few days ago, I recorded an oral history reflection while walking through a utility transmission corridor in Northern Illinois.

When I brought the footage home to my workstation, the audio was an unlistenable disaster. I opened a terminal with an AI coding assistant and started with a prompt that was as vague and blunt as it gets:

“Fix the buzz and hum in this video, and fix the sync drift.”

I didn’t give it filter coefficients. I didn’t specify high-pass cutoff frequencies, notch Q-factors, or container atom layouts. I just pointed it at a corrupted 3.1 GB file and asked it to fix the noise.

What followed over the next three days was a case study in what these tools can actually do, where they fail, and why they are so psychologically dangerous.

The First Trap: The Naive Algorithmic Fix

The AI started by inspecting the file with ffprobe. It noticed that the video track was 21 minutes and 43 seconds long, but the audio track was only 18 minutes and 11 seconds long.

It immediately jumped to a naive mathematical conclusion: The audio clock had drifted by 212 seconds.

So it divided 18 minutes by 21 minutes, calculated an atempo ratio of 0.837, stretched the audio into robotic slow motion, and reported victory.

If I had accepted that first answer at face value—like so many automated workflows do—the result would have been unwatchable garbage.

It took human skepticism to push back: “The sync really doesn’t work. Prove it.”

When we actually extracted lip-sync frames at 11 seconds in and aligned them against a Whisper speech transcript, the truth became obvious: 1. The speech was recorded at perfectly natural 1.0x speed. I had simply stopped talking at 18 minutes with “Thank you,” and the camera kept recording silent scenery for three and a half minutes while I walked. The audio had never drifted at all. 2. The phone used dual microphones (a bottom mic for voice and a top mic for noise cancellation). Under 345 kV transmission lines, the top mic acted like an inductive antenna, flooding the audio with electromagnetic noise. Simply isolating the clean Left channel and duplicating it across stereo eliminated over 95 percent of the drone before applying any DSP filters.

What Is Possible: The Perceptual Win

Once we steered the AI past its own false assumptions, it engineered a deterministic, open-source Bash script (fix_video.sh) using Homebrew tools: - High-pass filtering to cut handling rumble - Corona discharge impulse de-clicking - Spectral noise reduction - 10 harmonic Butterworth notches cutting 60 Hz buzz through 600 Hz - Downward expander gating during pauses - Bit-for-bit video passthrough without generational re-encoding

Automated speech recognition (whisper.cpp) went from broken gibberish to 93.3 percent word accuracy.

The restored video is live on YouTube: https://youtu.be/8LYHo1dOkMo

The Economic Ledger: Forty Bucks vs. Ten Thousand Dollars

On the machine side: Across 818 reasoning turns, 1,793 steps, 4.6 million prompt tokens, and 103 million cached context reads, the total raw compute cost was $39.40. That is pocket change on a flat $20/month subscription.

On the human side: Assembling the commercial specialists required to do this manually—a forensic audio engineer, a video codec specialist, a systems automation engineer, an ML engineer, and a tech lead—would take roughly 70 billable hours across two weeks. At standard contract rates, that is about $10,000 in specialized labor.

That is a 250 to 1 cost compression and a 10 to 1 speed compression.

This is why people are seduced by generative AI. You spend less than forty dollars and walk away with what looks like the output of a specialized five-person team.

What Is Just Slop? (The Hidden Damage)

So is this a miracle, or is it just sophisticated slop?

The honest answer: Perceptually it is a victory, but acoustically it is blunt-force trauma.

An LLM has no biological ears. It cannot feel sub-bass pressure or hear the warmth of a human voice. It pulls textbook formulas out of its training weights and optimizes for the happy path of noise reduction.

If you listen on flat studio reference monitors, the compromises are obvious: - Cascading 10 recursive notch filters smeared the phase in the low-mids, making the voice sound boxy. - Cutting frequencies above 4 kHz killed electrical sizzle, but also chopped off vocal sibilance like s, th, and f sounds. - Gating pauses by 91 percent sucked the natural outdoor room tone away, creating an unnatural pumping sensation. - Padding trailing silence with pure digital zeroes caused Whisper to hallucinate phantom phrases on empty space.

A professional audio mastering engineer would never use these blunt tools. If you ship this output without understanding these trade-offs, you are shipping slop with a shiny coat of paint.

The Competence Trap: The High-Thrown Ball

The central risk of generative AI is what I call the Dunning-Kruger Catapult:

“How high can I throw the ball before I lose sight of it, drop it, or have it come crashing straight down on my head?”

When a model gives you ten thousand dollars of specialized output from a vague prompt for forty bucks, your subconscious tricks you into believing that you possess that competence. You feel like an architect who has mastered physics, filter design, container timing, and speech models all at once.

You forget that you didn’t acquire that competence—you rented an approximation of it.

The danger is that you are competent enough to recognize improvement, but lack the domain depth to recognize subtle damage. If you don’t know where the math breaks down, the ball eventually comes down on your head in production.

The Compounding Debt of Good Enough

Beyond the audio file, there is an even bigger lesson: Evaluating “good enough” in isolation is a trap.

When people look at an AI demo, they evaluate the end result: “The audio sounds fine on my phone! Ship it.”

They ignore the scaffolding around it: - The transcription tool that hallucinates on silent gaps. - The shell script that assumes GNU flags and breaks on macOS. - The publishing pipeline where unescaped syntax breaks builds. - The broadcast setup that desyncs under display scaling.

If five components in your chain each have an 85 percent reliability rate, your total system reliability is 0.85 multiplied across all five stages—roughly 44 percent.

More than half the time, an unverified AI stack fails in production. The money saved upfront is deferred into continuous operational fires.

Inoculating Ourselves Against AI Psychosis

This isn’t written to call anyone out or give folks a hard time. If anything, I am diagnosing myself first.

When you sit at a terminal and watch a multi-variable problem get solved in an afternoon for the cost of lunch, a psychological shift happens. It is easy to slip into what some engineers call “AI psychosis”—a euphoric feeling that because the model answers quickly and plausibly from a simple prompt, the friction of reality has disappeared.

You start believing your own hype.

How do we inoculate ourselves against that? 1. Anchor in the physical baseline. Never throw away the raw recording, the raw trace, or the failing test. 2. Measure the compromises, not just the speed. For every win the AI gives you, look for what it broke or blurred to get there. 3. Invite open peer review. Don’t hide behind polished demos. Share the seams and let other humans verify the work.

That is why both the raw 3.1 GB corrupted master and the restored master are stored side-by-side in public storage and linked in the project repository.

An amateur hides the raw file because they fear comparison. A craftsman puts the unvarnished problem next to the automated solution, publishes the code, discloses the compromises, and invites people to inspect the seams.
