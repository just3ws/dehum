# LinkedIn Article: Under the High-Tension Wires

Title:
Under the High-Tension Wires: What a Ruined Field Recording Taught Me About AI Leverage, Slop, and Real Mastery

Article Body:

A quick disclosure before we start: Every line of code, audio filter, and word in this analysis was generated by AI (Gemini 3.8 Flash via Google's Antigravity pairing CLI).

The only human in this entire loop is me: the guy who walked out beneath 345 kV high-tension lines talking into a phone, and the guy sitting at the keyboard continually prodding and steering the model. Everything else is synthetic extrusion. We are not hiding the machine; we are laying it bare.


A few days ago, I recorded an oral history reflection while walking through a utility transmission corridor in Northern Illinois.

When I brought the footage home to my workstation, the file was an acoustic disaster:
- A deafening 60 Hz hum with 10 audible harmonics buzzing up to 600 Hz.
- Corona discharge clicks from high-voltage atmospheric ionization.
- A bizarre 212-second container discrepancy where the video was 21 minutes long and the audio was only 18 minutes.

In a traditional media production workflow, this footage would be tossed or scheduled for an expensive reshoot.

Instead, I opened the terminal and started pairing with Gemini.


The Game We Are Playing

We are living through a moment where anyone with a prompt can summon a choir of synthetic specialists.

The game many of us in engineering are playing right now is simple: we are stress-testing these tools on real, dirty, physical problems to see what actually holds up when the demo ends.

We aren't running synthetic code benchmarks. We took a corrupted real-world recording and asked three blunt questions:
1. What is genuinely possible?
2. What is just convincing slop?
3. Where is the competence trap when you can borrow ten thousand dollars of expertise for forty bucks?


What Is Possible: The Perceptual Win

Over the course of an afternoon, the model and I dismantled the root causes:

First, we debunked a false clock drift. The AI initially assumed the audio had desynced and suggested time-stretching. But checking lip-sync frame-by-frame proved the speech was at natural 1.0x speed. I had simply stopped talking at 18 minutes with "Thank you," and the camera kept recording silence.

Second, we found a hardware antenna issue. Smartphones use dual microphones: a voice mic at the bottom and a noise-cancelling mic at the top. Under 345 kV power lines, the top mic acted like an antenna, flooding the audio with electromagnetic noise. Simply isolating the clean Left channel and duplicating it across stereo eliminated over 95 percent of the drone before touching any filters.

Third, we built an open-source Bash script (fix_video.sh) using highpass filtering, click removal, spectral denoising, Butterworth harmonic notches, and expander gating.

Automated speech recognition (whisper.cpp) went from broken gibberish to 93.3 percent word accuracy. And we copied the video stream bit-for-bit without generational quality loss.

The restored video is live on YouTube: https://youtu.be/8LYHo1dOkMo


The Economic Ledger: Forty Bucks vs. Ten Thousand Dollars

Because this whole session was instrumented, we tracked the exact resource balance sheet:

On the machine side:
818 reasoning turns across 1,793 steps. About 4.6 million prompt tokens and 103 million cached context reads. Total raw compute cost was $39.40, which is basically pocket change on a flat developer subscription.

On the human side:
Assembling the commercial talent needed to do this manually—a forensic audio engineer, a video codec specialist, a systems automation engineer, an ML engineer, and a tech lead—would take roughly 70 billable hours across two weeks. At standard contract rates, that is about $10,000 in specialized labor.

That is a 250 to 1 cost compression and a 10 to 1 speed compression.

This is why people are seduced by generative AI. You spend less than forty bucks and walk away with what looks like the output of a specialized five-person team.


What Is Just Slop? (The Hidden Damage)

So is this a miracle, or is it just sophisticated slop?

The honest answer: Perceptually it is a victory, but acoustically it is blunt-force trauma.

An LLM has no biological ears. It cannot feel sub-bass pressure or hear the warmth of a human voice. It pulls textbook formulas out of its training weights and optimizes for the happy path of noise reduction.

If you listen on flat studio reference monitors, the compromises are obvious:
- Cascading 10 recursive notch filters smeared the phase in the low-mids, making the voice sound boxy.
- Cutting frequencies above 4 kHz killed the sizzle, but also chopped off vocal sibilance like s, th, and f sounds.
- Gating pauses by 91 percent sucked the natural outdoor room tone away, creating an unnatural pumping sensation.
- Padding trailing silence with pure digital zeroes caused Whisper to hallucinate phantom phrases on empty space.

A professional audio engineer would never use these blunt tools. If you ship this output without understanding these trade-offs, you are shipping slop with a shiny coat of paint.


The Competence Trap: The High-Thrown Ball

The central risk of generative AI is what I call the Dunning-Kruger Catapult:

How high can I throw the ball before I lose sight of it, drop it, or have it come crashing straight down on my head?

When a model gives you ten thousand dollars of specialized output for forty bucks, your subconscious tricks you into believing that you possess that competence. You feel like an architect who has mastered physics, filter design, container timing, and speech models all at once.

You forget that you didn't acquire that competence—you rented an approximation of it.

The danger is that you are competent enough to recognize improvement, but lack the domain depth to recognize subtle damage. If you don't know where the math breaks down, the ball eventually comes down on your head in production.


The Compounding Debt of Good Enough

Beyond the audio file, there is an even bigger lesson: Evaluating "good enough" in isolation is a trap.

When people look at an AI demo, they evaluate the end result: "The audio sounds fine on my phone! Ship it."

They ignore the scaffolding around it:
- The transcription tool that hallucinates on silent gaps.
- The shell script that assumes GNU flags and breaks on macOS.
- The publishing pipeline where unescaped syntax breaks builds.
- The broadcast setup that desyncs under display scaling.

If five components in your chain each have an 85 percent reliability rate, your total system reliability is 0.85 multiplied across all five stages—roughly 44 percent.

More than half the time, an unverified AI stack fails in production. The money saved upfront is deferred into continuous operational fires.


Inoculating Ourselves Against AI Psychosis

This isn't written to call anyone out or give folks a hard time. If anything, I am diagnosing myself first.

When you sit at a terminal and watch a multi-variable problem get solved in an afternoon for the cost of lunch, a psychological shift happens. It is easy to slip into what some engineers call "AI psychosis"—a euphoric feeling that because the model answers quickly and plausibly, the friction of reality has disappeared.

You start believing your own hype.

How do we inoculate ourselves against that?
1. Anchor in the physical baseline. Never throw away the raw recording, the raw trace, or the failing test.
2. Measure the compromises, not just the speed. For every win the AI gives you, look for what it broke or blurred to get there.
3. Invite open peer review. Don’t hide behind polished demos. Share the seams and let other humans verify the work.

That is why both the raw 3.1 GB corrupted master and the restored master are stored side-by-side in public storage and linked in the project repository.

An amateur hides the raw file because they fear comparison. A craftsman puts the unvarnished problem next to the automated solution, publishes the code, discloses the compromises, and invites people to inspect the seams.
